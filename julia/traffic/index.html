12.1.Traffic flow¶

Have you ever been driving on a highway when you suddenly came upon a traffic jam? Maybe you had to brake harder than you would like to admit, endured a period of bumper-to-bumper progress, and experienced a much more gradual emergence from dense traffic than the abrupt entry into it. The mathematics of this phenomenon are well understood.

Consider a one-dimensional road extending in the xxx direction. We represent the vehicles by a continuous density function ρ(x)\rho(x)ρ(x). The flow rate or flux of vehicles, expressed as the number of cars per unit time crossing a fixed point on the road, is denoted by qqq. We assume that this flux depends on the local density of cars. It’s reasonable to suppose that the flux will be zero when ρ=0\rho=0ρ=0 (no cars), reach a maximum qmq_mqm​ at some ρ=ρm\rho=\rho_mρ=ρm​, and approach zero again as the density approaches a critical density ρc\rho_cρc​. These conditions are met by the model

Q0(ρ)=4qmρmρ(ρ−ρc)(ρm−ρc)[ρ(ρc−2ρm)+ρcρm]2.Q_0(\rho) = \frac{4 q_m \rho_m \rho (\rho-\rho_c) (\rho_m-\rho_c)}{[\rho (\rho_c-2 \rho_m)+\rho_c \rho_m]^2}.Q0​(ρ)=[ρ(ρc​−2ρm​)+ρc​ρm​]24qm​ρm​ρ(ρ−ρc​)(ρm​−ρc​)​.
(12.1.1)

Observations (Whitham (1974), Chapter 3) suggest that good values for a three-lane highway are ρc=1080\rho_c = 1080ρc​=1080 vehicles per km, ρm=380\rho_m=380ρm​=380 vehicles per km, and qm=4500q_m=4500qm​=4500 vehicles per hour. In addition, we want to account for the fact that drivers anticipate slowing down or speeding up when they perceive changes in density, and therefore use

q=Q0(ρ)−ϵρxq=Q_0(\rho)-\epsilon \rho_xq=Q0​(ρ)−ϵρx​
(12.1.2)

for a small ϵ>0\epsilon > 0ϵ>0.

12.1.1Conservation laws¶

Conservation laws play a major role in science and engineering. They are typically statements that matter, energy, momentum, or some other meaningful quantity cannot be created or destroyed. In one dimension they take the form

ut+qx=0,u_t + q_x = 0,ut​+qx​=0,
(12.1.3)

where uuu represents the density of a conserved quantity, such as mass, and qqq represents the flux (flow rate) of u.u.u. Typically, the flux is a function of uuu, q=Q(u)q=Q(u)q=Q(u). By the chain rule,

ut+Q′(u)ux=0.u_t + Q'(u) u_x = 0.ut​+Q′(u)ux​=0.
(12.1.4)

For the particular Q0(ρ)Q_0(\rho)Q0​(ρ) for traffic flow, we arrive at the evolutionary PDE

ρt+Q0′(ρ)ρx=ϵρxx. \rho_t + Q_0'(\rho) \rho_x = \epsilon \rho_{xx}.ρt​+Q0′​(ρ)ρx​=ϵρxx​.
(12.1.5)

Note that Q0′Q_0'Q0′​ has the dimensions of (cars per time) over (cars per length), or length over time. We recognize the first and last terms as indicative of diffusion, but the middle term has a different effect. A similar term appears in the Black–Scholes equation.

12.1.2Advection equation¶

Let’s momentarily consider a simpler, more fundamental PDE.

Definition 12.1.1 (Advection equation)

The advection equation in one dimension is

ut+cux=0, u_t + c u_x = 0,ut​+cux​=0,
(12.1.6)

where ccc is constant.

The advection equation is the archetype of a hyperbolic PDE, which is a separate class from the parabolic PDEs.

It’s surprisingly easy to produce a solution of (12.1.6). Let u(x,t)=ψ(x−ct)u(x, t)=\psi(x-c t)u(x,t)=ψ(x−ct), where ψ\psiψ is any differentiable function of one variable. Then the chain rule says that

ut+cux=ψ′(x−ct)⋅(−c)+c[ψ′(x−ct)]=0,u_t + cu_x = \psi'(x-c t)\cdot(-c) + c [\psi'(x-c t)] = 0,ut​+cux​=ψ′(x−ct)⋅(−c)+c[ψ′(x−ct)]=0,
(12.1.7)

and the PDE is satisfied. Furthermore, u(x,0)=ψ(x)u(x,0) = \psi(x)u(x,0)=ψ(x), so the initial value of uuu determines ψ\psiψ. We have proved the following.

Theorem 12.1.1 (Advection equation)

If u0(x)u_0(x)u0​(x) is a continuously differentiable function, then the solution of the advection equation (12.1.6) satisfying u(x,0)=u0(x)u(x,0)=u_0(x)u(x,0)=u0​(x) is

u(x,t)=u0(x−ct),u(x,t) = u_0(x-c t),u(x,t)=u0​(x−ct),
(12.1.8)

if there are no boundary conditions.

The form of (12.1.8) tells us that uuu remains constant along any path on which x−ctx-c tx−ct is constant, i.e., x=a+ctx=a + c tx=a+ct for a constant aaa. Thus, if c>0c>0c>0, a fixed value of uuu moves rightward with speed ∣c∣|c|∣c∣, and if c<0c<0c<0, it moves leftward with speed ∣c∣|c|∣c∣.

Observation 12.1.1

Solutions to the advection equation propagate with constant speed and fixed shape.

Note

Keep in mind that ccc is a velocity, not a speed: if c>0c>0c>0, solutions travel rightward, and if c<0c<0c<0, they travel leftward.

Example 12.1.1 (Advection equation)

We solve the advection equation on [−4,4][-4,4][−4,4] with periodic end conditions using the method of lines.

12.1.3Solutions for traffic flow¶

Returning to the traffic flow PDE (12.1.5), we can interpret the conservation law as advection at velocity Q0′(ρ)Q_0'(\rho)Q0′​(ρ). The dependence of velocity on the solution is different from the linear PDE (12.1.6) and is the key to the peculiar behavior of traffic jams. In particular, the velocity is low at high density, and higher at low density. The term on the right side of (12.1.5) provides a bit of diffusion, and the parameter ϵ\epsilonϵ determines the balance between the two effects—advection effects dominate if ϵ\epsilonϵ is small.

Exact solutions of (12.1.5) are much harder to come by than for the standard advection equation, but the method of lines is still effective.

Example 12.1.2 (Traffic flow model)

We solve for traffic flow using periodic boundary conditions.

The following are parameters and a function relevant to defining the problem.

ρc = 1080;  ρm = 380;  q_m = 10000;
dQ0 = ρ -> 4q_m * ρc^2 * (ρc-ρm) * ρm * (ρm-ρ) / (ρ*(ρc-2*ρm) + ρc*ρm)^3;

Here we create a discretization on m=800m=800m=800 points.

x, Dₓ, Dₓₓ = FNC.diffper(800, [0, 4]);

Next we define the ODE resulting from the method of lines.

ode = (ρ, ϵ, t) -> -dQ0.(ρ) .* (Dₓ*ρ) + ϵ * (Dₓₓ*ρ);

Our first initial condition has moderate density with a small bump. Because of the diffusion present, we use a stiff solver for the IVP.

using OrdinaryDiffEqRosenbrock: Rodas4P
ρ_init = @. 400 + 10 * exp( -20*(x-3)^2 )
IVP = ODEProblem(ode, ρ_init, (0., 1.), 0.02)
sol = solve(IVP, Rodas4P());

In this case the density bump travels backward along the road. It also steepens on the side facing the incoming traffic and decreases much more slowly on the other side. A motorist would experience this as an abrupt increase in density, followed by a much more gradual decrease in density and resulting gradual increase in speed. (You also see some transient, high-frequency oscillations. These are caused by instabilities, as we discuss in simpler situations later in this chapter.)

The phenomenon in the second plot of Example 12.1.2 is called a shock wave. The underlying mathematics is not much different from the shock wave that comes off of the wing of a supersonic aircraft in the form of a sonic boom, or from cresting waves under certain conditions in the ocean. In the absence of diffusion (ϵ=0\epsilon=0ϵ=0), the shock becomes a jump discontinuity in the solution, which breaks down both the finite differences and the original PDE, requiring different approaches. For many applications, the addition of a small amount of diffusion is appropriate and simple. However, we will first try to come to terms with pure advection in a linear problem.
